In supervised learning, predictive models are constructed from datasets containing both input variables (features) and their corresponding output labels (target classes). The objective is to infer a functional relationship between predictors and outcomes that generalises to unseen data. A typical workflow for LSB detection involves an expert or rule-based criteria labelling LSB days (e.g., Section 3.2.2), followed by training a model on these labels. Supervised learning is therefore suited to tasks where labelled examples can be provided by experts or established criteria. However, a model's skill is measured against those labels. When the labels are derived from the same data or criteria as the model inputs, the score reflects agreement with the labelling rather than independent detection skill.

Only three studies have applied supervised ML to detect sea breezes from observations, and their reported skill values are not directly comparable. The studies differ in region and climate regime, label construction, sea-breeze frequency within the evaluation sample and validation design. We therefore interpret each result within its own study design. As a rough cross-study indication, we derive the true skill statistic (TSS) from the published confusion matrices: TSS = H − F, with H = a / (a + c) and F = b / (b + d), where a, b, c and d are the numbers of hits, false alarms, misses and correct negatives. TSS is largely independent of sea-breeze frequency (Doswell et al., 1990; Bloomfield et al., 2012). We also report precision, a / (a + b), which depends on local frequency and is therefore specific to each site.

Sokolov et al. (2020) tested four supervised classifiers for coastal meteorological events (sea breeze, fog, high wind and other): K-nearest neighbours (KNN), support vector machines (SVM), quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA) and the Parzen–Rosenblatt window (PRW). The inputs were 15-min ultrasonic-anemometer and weather-station measurements at Dunkirk, France. Events between July 2013 and August 2014 were classified manually by an expert, who identified 36 sea-breeze events from shifts in wind speed and direction, temperature, relative humidity and solar radiation, supported by Doppler lidar profiles. Sea breezes made up 2.7% of the samples. Using 10-fold block cross-validation to account for autocorrelation, KNN and QDA performed best, with no significant difference between them, detecting on average about 80% of events per class; this is a mean over all four classes, not a sea-breeze accuracy. For the sea-breeze class alone, the published confusion matrices imply a TSS of 0.41–0.74 and, at the observed class frequency, a precision of 5–12%. Horizontal wind speed and humidity were the most informative predictors overall, followed by wind direction and solar radiation. The authors noted that none of the algorithms directly accounted for the time dependence of the meteorological parameters.

Roy et al. (2022) developed four automated algorithms for detecting sea-breeze (SB) and nocturnal low-level jet (NLLJ) events from two years (2018–2019) of sonic-anemometer measurements at a nearshore site in Dunkirk. For SB days they used two methods:

- a rule-based method (M1), which applies a sequence of filters including a sign change of the normalised SBC (Section 3.1.1) and a temperature-slope check;
- an RNN-based long short-term memory network (NM2), which classifies each day from the 24-h series of 15-min SBC values.

NM2 was trained on the M1 labels for April–September 2018 and tested on April–September 2019 (182 days, 43% SB days), with a reported accuracy of 95% (derived TSS ≈ 0.89, precision ≈ 0.97). These scores measure how well NM2 reproduces the rule-based criteria rather than skill against an independent reference. An independent check during an 86-day campaign in 2021, using a different anemometer and Doppler lidar, found that NM2 detected 22 of 25 lidar-identified SB events (88%), although false detections were not reported.

Krishnamoorthy et al. (2023) applied rule-based criteria to one year of offshore lidar data from the Gulf of Khambhat, India, to label sea-breeze and non-sea-breeze cases. They then trained Random Forest, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting and SVM classifiers on these labels using a 70/30 train–test split. The labelling thresholds used wind direction, wind speed, turbulence intensity, relative humidity and time of day; wind direction, wind speed and relative humidity were also model inputs, together with temperature. XGBoost performed best (AUC 0.98, F1 0.92; derived TSS ≈ 0.96, precision 0.87). Because the model learns labels built largely from its own input variables, this score mainly reflects how well it reproduces the labelling criteria rather than skill against an independent reference.

All three studies use a single site, two of them the same site in Dunkirk, and none tests a trained model at another coastline or in another climate regime. Their transferability therefore remains untested, which motivates the common multi-site benchmark proposed in Recommendation v.

*Footnote:* TSS and precision derived by the authors from the confusion matrices in the cited studies. For Roy et al. (2022), class 2 in their Fig. 7c is assumed to denote sea breeze, consistent with the 78 SB days reported for 2019; TSS is unaffected by this assignment.
