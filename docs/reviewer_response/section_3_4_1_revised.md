## 3.4.1 Supervised learning

In supervised learning, predictive models are constructed from datasets containing both input variables (features) and their corresponding output labels (target classes). The fundamental objective is to infer a functional relationship between predictors and outcomes, enabling the model to generalise this mapping to unseen data. A typical workflow for LSB detection involves an expert or rule-based criteria labelling LSB days (e.g., Section 3.2.2), followed by training a model on these labelled data. A model's skill is therefore measured against those labels. When the labels are derived from the same data or criteria as the model inputs, the score reflects agreement with the labelling rather than independent detection skill.

Only three studies have applied supervised machine learning (ML) to detect sea breezes from observations (Table xx). Their reported skill values are not directly comparable, because the studies differ in region and climate regime, label construction, sea-breeze frequency and validation design; we therefore interpret each result within its own study design. As a rough cross-study indication, Table xx also gives the true skill statistic, TSS = H − F, where H = a / (a + c) is the hit rate, F = b / (b + d) is the false-alarm rate, and a, b, c and d are the numbers of hits, false alarms, misses and correct negatives. TSS is largely independent of sea-breeze frequency (Doswell et al., 1990; Bloomfield et al., 2012). The table also gives precision, a / (a + b), which depends on local frequency and is therefore specific to each site. Both were derived from the published confusion matrices.

Sokolov et al. (2020) tested four supervised classifiers on in-situ observations at Dunkirk, France: k-nearest neighbours (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA) and the Parzen–Rosenblatt window (PRW). The labels were expert classifications of sea breeze, fog, high wind and other events, supported by lidar profiles. KNN and QDA performed best, correctly classifying about 80% of events on average across the four classes (not sea breezes alone). Horizontal wind and humidity were the most informative predictors.

Roy et al. (2022) trained a recurrent neural network (RNN) with long short-term memory (LSTM) units on sea-breeze days labelled by their rule-based detector, using two years (2018–2019) of surface measurements at Dunkirk. Its 95% accuracy on a held-out year measures how well the network reproduces the rule-based detector; in an independent lidar check, it detected 22 of 25 sea-breeze events (88%).

Selvarajan et al. (2026) trained random forest, gradient boosting, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost; sequentially built, regularised decision trees, each correcting the errors of the previous ones) and SVM classifiers on rule-based sea-breeze labels from offshore lidar in the Gulf of Khambhat, India. XGBoost performed best, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.93, plausibly because tree-based threshold splits closely reproduce threshold-based labels. Since the labels were built largely from the model's input variables, the score mainly reflects how well the model reproduces the labelling criteria.

All three studies use a single site, two of them in Dunkirk, and none tests a model at another coastline, so their transferability remains untested (Recommendation v).

## 3.4.2 Unsupervised approaches

In unsupervised approaches, algorithms identify patterns and structures in data without predefined labels. Allagbe et al. (2025) applied such an approach over the Gulf of Guinea, West Africa, using 15-min visible Meteosat Second Generation images (December–February, 2013–2018). They first used a morphological snake, an active-contour image-segmentation algorithm that evolves a curve towards the edges of bright cloud objects without any training data, to detect the cloud lines associated with the SBF. They then fitted curves through the detected cloud-edge positions to describe the SBF's inland propagation, comparing linear, cubic polynomial, moving-average and kernel-weighted local polynomial regression (kwLPR). kwLPR fits low-order polynomials locally, giving nearby points more weight, and followed the detected positions most closely, with a mean absolute distance error of 0.0034° compared with 0.021° and 0.056° for the moving-average and cubic-polynomial fits. The method was demonstrated on two case days and applies only to fronts marked by visible cumulus lines.
